01What this covers

The PRD says what we are building and why. This document says how — the actual components, the data flowing between them, and the places most likely to break in a real store rather than in a demo. If you are deciding whether a technical choice is sound, this is the document to argue with.

Table and API specifics live in Data Model & API so this document can stay about shape and reasoning rather than field names.

02Components at a glance

Six pieces. Nothing on the device beyond recording, compressing, and uploading — every judgement happens server-side.

03Mobile app & recording

The single hardest requirement in the whole system lives here: recording audio continuously for an entire shift, while the phone is locked, in a pocket, doing nothing else the associate would notice.

Why React Native, and Android only

Two separate decisions worth keeping distinct. React Native was chosen for development speed — it is the framework we can iterate fastest in for a pilot, not a choice made to get iOS and Android from one codebase. Android is the only target for Phase 1. iOS's stricter limits on continuous background audio — session suspension under memory pressure, tighter background-execution budgets — make an 8-hour always-recording session a much harder and less predictable problem there than on Android. Rather than split effort solving that on both platforms at once, Phase 1 ships Android only. iOS is deferred, not ruled out; the same React Native codebase can extend to it later once the recording model is proven.

Recording behaviour

  • Start/end shift is an explicit associate action, not automatic. Recording only happens between these two taps.
  • Format: Opus at 16 kbps mono. Speech-optimized, roughly 11 MB for an 8-hour shift — see cost math.
  • Persistent notification while recording, required by Android's foreground service model for continuous audio capture, and useful for trust regardless — the associate always knows the state.
  • Pause is always available, with no justification required, and pausing is never itself logged as a signal against the associate — see recording people.
  • Local buffer first. Audio is written to local storage in rolling segments before upload, so a dead network never loses a segment, only delays it.
Needs verification, not assumed

Android's own background-execution story is not free of risk: aggressive OEM battery managers (Xiaomi, Oppo, and similar) kill background services well before stock Android would, and a foreground service with a notification does not fully protect against it in practice. We have not yet run an 8-hour on-body test across a spread of Android OEMs. Needs a real device spike before we commit further engineering — see Week 0.

04Upload & chunking

The app does not hold eight hours of audio and upload it all at shift end. It uploads in chunks roughly every two hours, for three reasons: it caps how much is lost if the app crashes or the phone dies, it keeps individual uploads small enough to survive patchy store Wi-Fi, and it lets transcription start before the shift is even over.

  • Trigger: time-based (every ~2h) and opportunistic (on reaching known store Wi-Fi), not continuous streaming.
  • Payload: a single chunk is roughly 2-3 MB Opus, far under Voicemaker's ~100 MB per-request limit, leaving headroom rather than sitting near the ceiling.
  • Retry: exponential backoff, resumable — a failed chunk is retried on the next window, not abandoned.
  • Ordering: chunks carry a sequence number and shift ID so segmentation (section 7) can reconstruct a continuous timeline even if chunks arrive out of order.

The polling interval is tunable per deployment — tighter for faster feedback, looser to save battery. Start conservative; tighten once we have real battery data.

05Backend on Supabase

Supabase gives us Postgres, auth, object storage, and serverless functions in one place, which matters for a three-person team trying to reach a pilot quickly rather than run infrastructure. Nothing here is exotic enough to justify assembling the equivalent ourselves.

What Supabase does for us
  • Auth for associates and managers, with row-level security scoping every query to a store
  • Object storage for raw audio chunks and, if we keep it, transcript text
  • Postgres as the single source of truth for shifts, conversations, rubrics, and scores
  • Edge functions as the glue: triggered on upload, they call Voicemaker, then segmentation, then the scoring LLM
What we are watching
  • Edge function cold starts and timeout limits under a full processing chain — may need to split into a queue rather than one long function
  • Storage egress cost if audio retention turns out to be long — see retention decision
  • Row-level security policy correctness — a manager must never see another store's data by accident

06Transcription & diarization

Each uploaded chunk goes to Voicemaker's flagship model. Two things matter more than raw accuracy: telling associate from customer, and handling code-mixed Hindi-English speech, which is the normal register of retail conversation in most Indian cities.

  • Diarization is not yet enabled on Voicemaker's side for multi-channel input — Sujit is enabling it. Until then, transcripts have text but not a reliable speaker label, which blocks most of the rubric.
  • Language: Hindi and English for the first deployment. Dialectal variation — Punjabi-accented Hindi is the flagged example — is an accuracy gap we have not measured yet.
  • Real-time is not available. Transcription happens after a chunk is fully uploaded, which is consistent with feedback being end-of-shift rather than live, and matches the product decision to never coach mid-conversation.
  • No on-device transcription. Ruled out on the expectation that quantized on-device models would lose too much accuracy on this kind of speech, and that mobile compute budget is tight regardless. This was a judgement call, not a measured result — nobody has benchmarked it. Ruled out for now, not forever.
Everything about Voicemaker here is unverified

The API documentation has not been shared with engineering yet — it is still an open action on Sujit. The payload ceiling, the "flagship model" designation, the absence of real-time, and the diarization behaviour described in this document all come from a verbal summary in one meeting, not from docs anyone has read. Confirm the vendor's speech-to-text capability and limits before building against them; do not quote these figures to the customer.

07Conversation segmentation

A shift transcript is a wall of text spanning eight hours. Segmentation cuts it into individual customer conversations — the unit the rubric actually scores.

  • Signal: silence gaps above a threshold, diarization turn-taking patterns, and a minimum-length filter to discard stray small talk between colleagues.
  • Failure modes to design against: merging two customers into one scored conversation (dilutes and confuses the rubric), or splitting one customer into two (scores an incomplete interaction as if it were complete).
  • Output: a list of conversation records, each with a start/end timestamp into the shift audio and a diarized transcript, ready for scoring.

This stage is the one most dependent on diarization quality — see the open questions table for where that stands.

08Scoring pipeline

Each segmented conversation is sent to an LLM along with the store's rubric. The model returns a score per rubric item, a short justification, and a supporting quote from the transcript — never a bare number. The exact prompt structure and output schema are in Data Model & API.

  • Scoring is asynchronous and can happen as soon as a conversation is segmented — it does not wait for the whole shift to end.
  • A conversation with too little audio, or where the customer's side is inaudible, should be flagged as unscoreable rather than forced through the rubric. A confident-looking wrong score is worse than an honest gap.
  • Per-associate daily scores are an aggregate of the day's scoreable conversations, computed after the last one lands.

09Feedback delivery

Once the daily aggregate and message are generated, delivery goes through the app and, pending WhatsApp Business API template approval, WhatsApp — see the feedback rules in the PRD for tone and content constraints. Manager dashboards are a weekly pull, computed from the same underlying scores, not a separate pipeline.

10Failure handling

The system will fail in ordinary ways constantly — dropped uploads, a third-party API timeout, a phone dying at 40% battery. None of these should be surprising in production.

FailureBehaviour
Chunk upload failsStays in local buffer, retried with backoff on the next window. Nothing is deleted from device until upload is confirmed.
Voicemaker request times out or errorsRequeued with backoff; after N failures, the chunk is marked failed and surfaced in an internal ops view rather than silently dropped.
Segmentation finds no clear boundariesFalls back to treating the whole chunk as one long conversation rather than discarding it, flagged as low-confidence.
Scoring LLM output fails schema validationOne retry with the same input; if it fails again, the conversation is marked unscored, not silently zeroed.
Associate ends shift mid-uploadAny buffered chunk still uploads; end-of-shift does not cancel in-flight work.

11Security & data handling

  • Row-level security in Postgres scopes every query to the associate's own data or the manager's own store — enforced at the database layer, not just in application code.
  • Audio in transit and at rest is encrypted (TLS for upload, storage-provider encryption at rest).
  • PII stripping runs on transcripts before long-term storage — phone numbers, names, card numbers, addresses — per the PRD's privacy commitments.
  • Retention: transcripts deleted after 90 days by a scheduled job; whether raw audio is kept at all is still an open decision — see open questions.
  • Access: nobody browses raw recordings by default. Playback is gated behind an explicit dispute-review flow, logged when used.

13Open technical questions

Full list with owners and target dates lives in the roadmap's open questions table. The ones with the largest architectural blast radius:

Blocking
  • Multi-channel diarization is not live yet — segmentation and scoring both depend on it.
  • Audio quality from a pocketed phone mic is unproven — see Week 0.
Design-affecting
  • Continuous vs. triggered recording changes the upload and battery model.
  • Audio retention decision changes storage cost and the security surface materially.
