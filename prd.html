01Why this exists

Imagine two people selling the same phone in the same store on the same day. One sells nine. The other sells two. Everyone assumes the first person is just better. Nobody can say what they actually did differently, because nobody was listening.

That is the entire problem. In a physical store, the most important thing that happens — the conversation between an associate and a customer — leaves no record. It happens, it affects whether a sale occurs, and then it evaporates.

Because there is no record, three things follow:

Coaching is guesswork

A manager coaches based on what they happened to overhear, or on a gut feeling about someone. It is not wrong, exactly. It is just unevidenced.

Good habits do not spread

When someone finds a line that works, it stays with them. There is no mechanism to notice it, name it, and teach it to the other eleven people on the floor.

Training is unmeasurable

A retailer spends real money on training and cannot tell whether anyone changed their behaviour afterwards. Sales moved, but sales always move.

Every one of these is solved in a call centre, and has been for twenty years, because calls are recorded. The shop floor never got that. We are trying to give it that, and then do something useful with it.

02The end goal

In one sentence

Every retail associate should finish their shift knowing one specific thing they did well and one specific thing to do differently tomorrow — based on what they actually said, not on someone's impression of them.

That is the whole product. Everything else is machinery in service of that sentence.

Three things have to be true for it to be worth building:

  1. The feedback has to be specific. "Improve your product knowledge" is useless. "A customer asked about battery life twice and you moved to price both times" is something a person can act on.
  2. It has to arrive quickly. Feedback about Tuesday delivered on Friday is a performance review. Feedback about Tuesday delivered on Tuesday evening is coaching.
  3. The associate has to not hate it. If it feels like surveillance, they will find a way to defeat it, and they will be right to. It has to feel like something that helps them earn more.

03Who uses it

PersonWhat they wantWhat they getWhat worries them
The associate
Primary user		 To sell more, hit incentives, not be embarrassed in front of colleagues A short private message at the end of each shift: one strength, one fix "Is this going to be used to fire me?"
The store manager
Secondary		 To know who needs help this week without hovering over everyone A weekly view of the team, sorted by who is struggling and with what "Is this more admin work for me?"
The regional / L&D head
Buyer		 To prove training spend produces behaviour change Trend lines across stores, before and after training interventions "Will this create a legal problem for us?"
Note the tension

The person who buys this and the person who uses it are not the same, and they do not want the same thing. The buyer wants oversight. The user wants help. If we build purely for the buyer we get a compliance tool that associates sabotage. The product has to be genuinely useful to the associate first, or it dies on the floor regardless of who signed the contract.

04A day in the life

Concretely, here is what the product looks like from the associate's side.

09:45 — Shift start
Ravi clocks in and opens the app

One tap on "Start shift". The app confirms it is recording with a persistent notification. He puts the phone in his shirt pocket and forgets about it.

10:00 – 18:00 — On the floor
He works a normal day

He talks to maybe twenty customers. He takes a lunch break and pauses recording with one tap. Roughly every two hours, when the store Wi-Fi is reachable, the app quietly sends what it has recorded. He does not notice this happening.

18:00 — Shift end
He taps "End shift"

Recording stops. Anything not yet uploaded goes now. Phone is at 38%, which is survivable but not great.

18:20 — Feedback
A message arrives

"Today you had 18 conversations. In 15 of them you asked what the customer would mainly use the phone for — that is your strongest habit and it is above the store average. In 6 conversations the customer raised price and you moved straight to the EMI option without acknowledging the concern. Tomorrow, try naming the concern first: 'I understand, it's a big spend.' Then offer EMI."

Monday — Manager
Priya reviews the week

She sees that four of her nine associates are weak on the same thing — handling price objections. That is a team huddle topic, not four separate conversations.

05What the product does

Broken into the five things it must do, in order.

5.1 — Record the conversation

The app records audio while the associate is on shift. Not video, not location, just audio, and only between "start shift" and "end shift". The associate can pause at any time and the app makes it obvious when it is recording. Recording must survive the phone being locked and put in a pocket, which is the single hardest technical requirement in the product.

5.2 — Turn it into text

Audio goes to our servers and is converted to a transcript. The transcript needs to mark who said what — associate or customer — because "did the associate ask a question" is meaningless if we cannot tell who was speaking. Conversations happen in a mix of Hindi and English, frequently within the same sentence, which is normal in Indian retail and difficult for speech systems.

5.3 — Split it into conversations

Eight hours of audio is not one conversation, it is twenty conversations separated by silence, walking, and small talk with colleagues. The system has to find the boundaries and discard everything that is not a customer interaction. Getting this wrong in either direction is bad: merge two customers and the scoring is nonsense; split one customer in half and we score an incomplete interaction.

5.4 — Score each conversation

Each conversation is evaluated against a rubric — a checklist of behaviours the retailer believes make a good interaction. See section 6.

5.5 — Say something useful

Scores are not the product. The product is a short message that a tired person will read on their commute home and remember one thing from. See section 7.

06What "good" means

The retailer defines this, not us

We do not know what good selling looks like in a jewellery store versus a phone store versus a pharmacy. The retailer does. We provide a starting template and the tools to edit it. They own the definition. This is a deliberate product decision, not laziness — a rubric the retailer did not write is a rubric their managers will not defend.

A rubric is a list of things to check for. A reasonable starting template for electronics retail:

BehaviourWhat we look for in the transcriptWeight
Greeting Did the associate speak first, within roughly the first 30 seconds, with a warm opening rather than "yes?" 10%†
Needs discovery Did they ask what the customer actually needs — use case, budget, who it is for — before recommending anything? 25%†
Product knowledge Were the customer's questions answered accurately and confidently, or deflected? 20%†
Objection handling When the customer hesitated on price or features, was the concern acknowledged before being answered? 20%†
Add-ons Was a relevant accessory, warranty or exchange mentioned — naturally, not as a script? 10%†
Close Was there a clear next step, even when the customer left without buying? 15%†

Each behaviour gets a score, a short justification, and a quote from the transcript that supports it. The quote is not optional. A score without evidence is an opinion, and an associate who disagrees with an opinion has no way to check it.

On the word "ideal"

The customer will want to define an "ideal call". Worth being careful here: no real conversation matches an ideal, and scoring everyone against a perfect script produces uniformly low scores that demotivate everyone. The rubric should describe behaviours that are present or absent, not proximity to a fictional perfect call.

07The feedback itself

This is the part of the product that people actually experience, so it deserves more care than the pipeline that produces it.

Rules
  • Under 80 words. It is read on a bus.
  • Exactly one strength and one improvement. Not three of each.
  • Quote what they actually said.
  • The improvement includes what to say instead.
  • Compare to their own past, not to the top performer.
  • Neutral tone. Not a cheerleader, not a manager.
Never
  • A bare number with no explanation.
  • A ranking against named colleagues.
  • Anything that reads as a warning or a threat.
  • Feedback on a day with too little usable audio — say nothing instead.
  • Comment on accent, personality, or anything not in the rubric.
  • More than one message per day.

Delivery channel

WhatsApp is where these users already are, and open rates will be far higher than an app notification. It also means the associate does not need to open our app to get value from it, which matters. The cost is that WhatsApp is a less controlled channel and message templates need approval. We should support both and measure which gets read.

08The manager view

A weekly dashboard, not a live one. A live dashboard invites a manager to watch people in real time, which turns the product into surveillance and destroys the associate's trust in it.

The manager should be able to answer four questions in under a minute:

  1. Who on my team is improving and who is sliding?
  2. What is the one behaviour my whole team is weakest on this week?
  3. Who has enough data for me to trust these numbers at all?
  4. What should I say in Monday's huddle?
Coverage must be visible

If an associate only produced two usable conversations this week, their score is meaningless and the dashboard must say so loudly rather than showing a number. The fastest way to lose the manager's trust is to let them act on a score built from noise.

09What success looks like

Figures marked † are placeholders

They were drafted to make the shape of the problem concrete, not derived from measurement or agreed with the customer. Nothing marked † should be quoted externally or planned against until it has been checked. Hover any marker to see this note again.

For the pilot, we are not measuring revenue. Attributing a sales change to a coaching tool over a few weeks in one store is not credible, and claiming it would damage our own case. We are measuring whether the machine works and whether people use it.

QuestionMeasurePilot target
Does it capture anything usable?Share of recorded audio that yields a scoreable conversation> 60%†
Do associates keep it on?Recorded hours ÷ scheduled shift hours> 70%†
Is the transcription good enough?Word error rate on a hand-checked sample< 25%†
Do the scores mean anything?Agreement between system score and a manager scoring the same conversation blind> 75%†
Does anyone read it?Feedback messages opened> 50%†
Is it fair?Associate-raised disputes about a score< 10%†
Would they miss it?Associates who say they would keep using it, asked at week 4> 50%†
Kill criteria

If usable-capture stays under 30% after we have tried mic placement fixes, or if score agreement with a human manager is under 50%, the product does not work and no amount of interface polish will save it. Better to establish that in six weeks than six months.

10What we are not doing

Explicitly out of scope for the first version. Listed so nobody assumes otherwise.

Not doingWhy not
Live coaching during a conversationRequires real-time transcription, which our provider does not offer yet. Also questionable — an earpiece telling you what to say makes you worse at talking to people.
Attributing sales to conversationsNeeds POS integration and a way to link a specific conversation to a specific transaction. Real, valuable, and a whole project on its own.
Video or body languageEnormously more intrusive for a marginal signal.
Emotion or sentiment scoringUnreliable across cultures and accents, and easy to weaponise against people. Deliberately excluded.
Dedicated hardware or lapel micsPhase 2, and only if the phone mic proves inadequate — which it might.
Languages beyond Hindi and EnglishLater. Get the pipeline right on two first.
Automated performance managementNobody gets fired by an algorithm. This is a coaching tool. Making it an HR tool would destroy adoption.

11Recording people

We are recording customers who did not download our app and have no relationship with us. This is the part most likely to become a problem, and it deserves to be handled properly rather than deferred.

What we will do for the pilot

  • Visible notice at the store entrance stating that conversations may be recorded for training and quality purposes. Same principle as CCTV signage, in English and the local language.
  • Associates sign a consent form and can pause recording at any moment with no justification required.
  • Personal details get stripped from transcripts before storage — phone numbers, names, card numbers, addresses.
  • Transcripts are deleted after 90 days. Only aggregate scores persist.
  • Nobody browses recordings. Managers see scores and summaries, not raw transcripts, except in a specific dispute where the associate asks for review.
Honest position

India's DPDP Act creates real obligations around notice and consent for personal data, and a recorded voice is personal data. Signage-based notice is a defensible position for a controlled pilot in a store the retailer owns, with the retailer as data fiduciary. It is not a position I would want to defend at national rollout scale. We should get an actual legal review before the second customer, not the tenth.

The associate's view of it

The thing that determines whether this product survives contact with real users is whether associates believe it exists to help them. Three commitments, made in writing at onboarding:

  • Their individual scores are visible to them and their direct manager. Nobody else.
  • Scores are never used for termination, and this is stated in the retailer's contract with us.
  • They can pause recording whenever they want, and pausing is never itself measured.

That third one costs us data. It is worth it. An associate who cannot turn it off will instead leave the phone in a locker, and then we get nothing and do not know why.

12How this could fail

The five most likely ways this does not work, stated plainly.

1 The audio is unusable

A phone in a shirt pocket picks up the associate's chest resonance clearly and the customer, standing four feet away in a noisy store, barely at all. Since half the rubric depends on the customer's side, this breaks everything. Most likely failure mode.

2 Nobody keeps it running

Battery drain, forgetting to start, or quiet resistance to being recorded. Coverage drops to the point where the data represents only the most compliant staff.

3 The scores feel wrong

An associate reads feedback about a conversation they remember going well, disagrees, and stops believing any of it. Trust is lost once and does not come back.

4 It becomes a stick

Despite our commitments, a regional manager uses the scores in an appraisal. Word spreads across stores in days and the tool is dead as a coaching product.

5 It works but nobody changes

Feedback is accurate, delivered, and read — and behaviour stays the same, because knowing what to do differently was never the bottleneck. Motivation was.

Mitigation

Failures 1–3 are testable in weeks with small effort. Failure 4 is contractual. Failure 5 is the one we can only learn by running a real pilot long enough to see whether scores move. Sequence the work accordingly.
