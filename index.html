Coaching retail associates
from the conversations they actually have.

Project Nobel records what happens on the shop floor, turns it into a transcript, scores it against a rubric the retailer defines, and sends the associate a short, specific piece of feedback every day. Managers get a weekly view of who is improving and who needs help.

02The problem

In e-commerce, every step of a sale is measured. In a physical store, almost none of it is. A retailer knows what was sold and what the footfall was. They do not know what the associate actually said, whether they asked the customer what they needed, whether they knew the product, or whether they even greeted the person who walked in.

Today the only tool is a manager standing nearby, or a mystery shopper visiting once a quarter. Both are rare, subjective, and too late to change anything. So coaching in retail is mostly opinion delivered occasionally, and nobody can tell whether it worked.

The one-line version

Retail has no equivalent of a call recording. We are trying to create one, and then make it useful to the person who was on the call.

04Bets we are making

These are assumptions, not facts. Each one is something the pilot is designed to test. If any of the first three is wrong, the product does not work in its current form.

#The betIf it is wrongConfidence
1 A phone mic in a pocket or lanyard picks up the customer clearly enough to transcribe. Whole approach needs external mics. Cost and rollout friction go up sharply. Low
2 Transcription is accurate enough in mixed Hindi–English retail speech to score fairly. Scores become noise. Associates stop trusting the feedback within a week. Medium
3 Associates will keep the app running rather than quietly killing it. Coverage collapses. We measure only the people who already comply. Medium
4 An LLM can apply a retail rubric about as consistently as a decent human manager. Fall back to a much narrower rubric: presence/absence checks only, no quality judgement. High
5 Daily specific feedback actually changes behaviour on the floor. Product is a measurement tool, not a coaching tool. Different, smaller business. Medium
Bet 1 is the one to settle first

Audio quality gates everything downstream. No amount of model quality fixes a recording where the customer is inaudible. Before writing pipeline code we should spend two days recording real store audio on a phone and listening to it. See Week 0 in the roadmap.

06Decided vs. open

Decided
  • React Native for the app, chosen for development speed, not cross-platform reach.
  • Android only for Phase 1. iOS's background-recording limits are a harder problem; deferred, not ruled out.
  • Supabase for backend, auth, database, storage and functions.
  • Voicemaker for transcription, flagship model.
  • Server-side diarization. Nothing about speaker separation on the device.
  • No on-device transcription. Quantized models lose too much accuracy.
  • Hindi and English for the first deployment.
  • Phone mic only in Phase 1. External hardware is Phase 2.
  • The retailer writes the rubric. We supply a starting template, they own it.
Open
  • Do we keep the audio after transcription, or only the transcript?
  • Continuous vs. triggered recording — always-on, or associate taps to start?
  • Consent mechanism for the customer. Signage alone, or something explicit?
  • Feedback channel — in-app, WhatsApp, or both from day one?
  • Diarization quality — unproven until Voicemaker multi-channel is live.
  • Dialect accuracy — Punjabi-accented Hindi is a known gap, untested.
  • Scoring transparency — does the associate see the transcript behind a score?
  • Battery cost of an 8-hour recording session, unmeasured.

Every open item has an owner and a target date in the open questions table.
